Best CarPlay Head Units for 2024 | Comparison Guide

Find the best CarPlay head unit for your car. Compare wired vs wireless, screen types, fit and features, plus install tips to choose with confidence.

Quick answer

The best CarPlay head unit is the one that fits your dash, connects the way you prefer, and keeps your steering wheel controls and camera working. For most drivers, a double-DIN touchscreen from an established brand is the safest pick. Confirm fit before comparing features.

Key takeaways

  • Dash fit comes first: check whether your car takes a single-DIN or double-DIN unit, or needs a floating-screen design.
  • Wireless CarPlay is convenient for short trips, while wired CarPlay is simpler and keeps your phone charging.
  • A capacitive touchscreen, a backup camera input and a physical volume control matter more day to day than long spec lists.
  • Plan for the install parts: a dash kit, wiring harness, antenna adapter and, if needed, a steering wheel control interface.
  • Buy from a known brand with a clear return policy and firmware support, and check compatibility with your exact vehicle.

Start with your dash, not the feature list

Most shoppers compare screens and features first, then discover the unit does not fit. Reverse that order. The physical opening in your dashboard decides which head units are realistic for your car.

Single-DIN, double-DIN and floating screens

A single-DIN opening is the shorter, older standard. Many single-DIN CarPlay units use a motorized fold-out screen or a large screen that hangs in front of the dash. A double-DIN opening is twice as tall and fits a standard fixed touchscreen flush in the dash. A floating screen mounts a larger display in front of the dash and is often the answer when your car has an awkward opening or you want a bigger display than the opening allows.

Floating screens can interfere with climate controls, hazard buttons or vents in some vehicles. Check the mounting depth and the position of nearby controls before you buy.

What to look up for your specific car

  • Opening size: single-DIN, double-DIN or a custom shape.
  • Dash kit: a trim piece that makes the new unit look factory-finished.
  • Wiring harness: a vehicle-specific adapter so you do not cut factory wires.
  • Antenna adapter: many cars need one for AM/FM reception.
  • Steering wheel control interface: keeps your volume and track buttons working.
  • Factory features: some cars route chimes, climate settings or amplifier signals through the stock radio, which can complicate a swap.

Wired vs. wireless CarPlay

This is the biggest functional choice after fit. Both run the same CarPlay interface on your iPhone. The difference is how the phone connects.

Wired CarPlay

Wired units use a USB cable. Your phone charges while you drive, the connection is generally straightforward, and the unit often costs less than a wireless equivalent. The downsides are cable clutter and plugging in every time you get in the car.

Wireless CarPlay

Wireless units pair over Bluetooth and Wi-Fi, so your phone connects automatically when you start the car. That suits short, frequent trips. Be aware that wireless use can drain the phone faster and warm it up, so a wireless charging pad or a charging cable on long drives may still be worth having.

Which should you choose?

  • Choose wireless if you take many short trips and want your phone to stay in a pocket or bag.
  • Choose wired if you take long drives, stream a lot of navigation and music, or want the simplest and most dependable setup.
  • Choose a unit that supports both if you share the car with others who may use different phones or cables.

Check Apple’s current list of supported iPhones and the head unit maker’s notes to confirm your phone works with the connection type you want.

Features that actually matter

Spec sheets can be long. These are the points that tend to affect daily use.

Screen quality and controls

  • Capacitive touchscreen: responds like a phone screen and is generally more pleasant than older resistive panels.
  • Brightness and glare: check reviews for readability in direct sun, especially on floating screens.
  • Physical volume knob or buttons: easier to use while driving than tapping a screen.
  • Screen resolution and size: bigger is not always better if it blocks other controls.

Inputs and outputs

  • Backup camera input: essential if you want a reversing view on the new screen.
  • Extra camera inputs: useful for front or side cameras or a dash camera feed.
  • Preamp outputs: important if you plan to add an amplifier or subwoofer later.
  • Microphone: some units include one, others need an external mic placed well. Poor placement hurts call and voice-assistant quality.

Software and support

  • Android Auto support: helpful if anyone else uses the car with an Android phone.
  • Firmware updates: a maker that publishes updates can fix bugs after purchase.
  • Audio tuning: an equalizer and time alignment help if you have upgraded speakers.

Remember that CarPlay uses your iPhone for maps, music and messaging. The head unit shows and controls the phone’s apps, and your phone’s cellular connection supplies the data.

Installation: DIY or professional?

A head unit swap is a realistic DIY project for many older vehicles if you are comfortable with basic wiring and removing trim panels. With the right harness and dash kit, it can be mostly plug-and-play.

Consider a professional installer if:

  • Your car has a factory amplifier, a fiber-optic audio system or integrated climate controls.
  • You need to install a floating screen with custom mounting.
  • You want a backup camera, microphone or extra inputs routed cleanly.
  • Your steering wheel controls need a programmable interface.

Whichever route you take, disconnect the battery before working on wiring, keep the original harness so you can return the car to stock, and test every function before reassembling the dash. That includes sound from each speaker, the reversing camera, steering wheel buttons and microphone.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying before checking fit: confirm the opening, depth and required adapters first.
  • Skipping the microphone plan: the factory mic may not work with an aftermarket unit.
  • Choosing on screen size alone: a large display that hides your hazard button or vents is a poor trade.
  • Ignoring return policies: if the unit does not suit your car, you want an easy return path.
  • Buying an unknown low-cost unit without support: check reviews, warranty terms and whether firmware updates are provided. Some very cheap units run their own software rather than true CarPlay, so confirm the listing says it supports Apple CarPlay.

A simple way to decide

  1. Identify your dash opening and any factory systems you must keep.
  2. Pick wired, wireless or both based on your trips and charging habits.
  3. Choose the type that fits: double-DIN, single-DIN or floating screen.
  4. Check for a backup camera input, a volume control and any outputs you will need later.
  5. Confirm the install parts list and whether you will do it yourself.
  6. Buy from a seller with a clear return and warranty policy.

Bottom line

The best CarPlay head unit is not a single model for everyone. It is the unit that physically fits your dash, uses the connection style that suits your driving, and keeps the features you rely on, such as a reversing camera and steering wheel controls. Start with fit, choose wired or wireless, then compare screen quality and inputs from established brands. Get the install parts right and your car will feel like a much newer one.

Frequently asked questions

Does CarPlay on a head unit use my phone's data plan?

Yes. CarPlay runs apps from your iPhone, so streaming music and live navigation use your phone's cellular data unless you download content for offline use. The head unit itself usually does not need its own data connection.

Can I add CarPlay if my car already has a factory screen?

Sometimes. Some vehicles can take a replacement unit, and others can use an add-on interface that works with the factory display. Options depend on the make, model and year, so check vehicle-specific compatibility before buying.

Will wireless CarPlay drain my iPhone battery?

It can, because the phone keeps Bluetooth and Wi-Fi active while also running maps or music. On longer drives, many people plug in a cable or use a wireless charging pad to keep the battery topped up.

Are very cheap CarPlay screens worth it?

Be cautious. Some low-cost units have weaker screens, limited support or software that is not true CarPlay. Look for a clear warranty, return policy, user reviews and a listing that explicitly states Apple CarPlay support.

Will my steering wheel controls still work after the swap?

Often yes, but you usually need a steering wheel control interface that matches both your car and the new head unit. Check that the unit supports one before you buy.

Do I need a professional to install a CarPlay head unit?

Not always. Many older cars allow a DIY install with the correct dash kit and harness. A professional is a better choice if your car has a factory amplifier, integrated controls or needs a custom floating-screen mount.
